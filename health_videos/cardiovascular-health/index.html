How arteries, cholesterol, plaques and blood clots shape the risk of heart attack and stroke.

From Plaque to Clot: How Heart Attacks and Strokes Happen

3:41 · Narrated, with on-screen captions

Heart attacks and many strokes begin the same way. Cholesterol builds up inside an artery wall and forms a plaque. When the thin fibrous cap over that plaque tears, the blood reacts as if to a wound and a clot forms. If the clot blocks a coronary artery, the result is a heart attack. If it blocks blood flow to the brain, the result is an ischemic stroke.

Read the transcript

Heart attacks and many strokes share the same story: a fatty plaque inside an artery, and a blood clot that blocks it.

This is a healthy artery. Its smooth inner lining, the endothelium, lets blood flow freely. Red blood cells carry oxygen, and tiny platelets circulate, ready to seal any injury.

When there is too much LDL cholesterol in the blood, LDL particles slip through the lining and get trapped inside the artery wall. There they become oxidized, and the body treats them as a threat.

The lining raises an alarm. White blood cells called monocytes stick to it and crawl into the wall. Inside, they become macrophages and swallow the cholesterol, until they are so full they turn into foam cells. This is the earliest stage, a fatty streak. It can begin as early as our teens and twenties.

Over the years, foam cells die and spill their fat, forming a soft core of lipid and debris. Muscle cells move in and build a fibrous cap over it. Together, this is a plaque. As it grows, the plaque bulges into the artery and narrows it, often without any symptoms.

Inflammation slowly weakens the cap. Macrophages release enzymes that digest its collagen, and the cap becomes thin. Then, often during a surge in blood pressure, the thin cap tears. This is plaque rupture. So it is not the cholesterol itself that bursts. It is the cap covering it that breaks open.

The tear exposes the plaque core to the blood, and the body reacts as if to a wound. Platelets stick to the tear, activate, and clump together. At the same time, the clotting cascade spins threads of fibrin that trap red blood cells. Within minutes, a clot, or thrombus, has formed.

If the clot grows large enough, it blocks the artery completely. Blood stops flowing, and the tissue downstream is starved of oxygen.

When this happens in a coronary artery, which feeds the heart muscle, the result is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction. Within about half an hour, heart muscle cells start to die, and the damage spreads the longer the artery stays closed.

When blood flow to part of the brain is blocked, the result is an ischemic stroke, the most common type of stroke. A plaque in a neck artery, such as the carotid, can rupture the same way. Pieces of the clot can break off and travel up into the brain. Clots can also form inside the heart itself, for example with an irregular rhythm called atrial fibrillation, and then travel to the brain. In a major stroke, close to two million brain cells can be lost every minute.

That is why every minute counts. Warning signs of a heart attack include chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, and pain spreading to the arm, jaw, or back. For stroke, remember BE FAST: Balance loss, Eye trouble, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call for help. Doctors can reopen the artery with clot-dissolving medicine, a stent, or by removing the clot, but treatment works best when it starts quickly.

The best treatment is prevention: keep LDL cholesterol and blood pressure in check, don't smoke, stay active, and manage blood sugar. Healthy arteries keep oxygen flowing to the heart and the brain.
